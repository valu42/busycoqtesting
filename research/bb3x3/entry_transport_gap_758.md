# What still separates the entry tests from a blank-tape proof

The [complete rectangle](wider_even_entry_758.md) and
[extended filtered test](entry_transport_758.md) certify the future of
specified ordinary counter states. To decide the blank machine using
them, one must establish that its actual trajectory reaches a certified
state or a reset in the halting basin. The population covered by a test
does not establish that reachability statement.

Two exact examples rule out plausible general shortcuts. Both begin at
specified reset configurations. **Neither starting reset is claimed to
be reachable from blank.** The point is to test purported implications
about arbitrary reset-origin trajectories, using the original counter
map rather than a distributional model.

## A recent reset need not give a small primitive crossing coordinate

Put $B=H-F$, the lower end of the established reset basin. Start at
$A(1,B-586)$. After 292 jumps and two ordinary bulks, its first odd/odd
state at or beyond the mass-coordinate crossing $u=B$ has

$$
u=B+3,\qquad
h=\frac{9\cdot3^{292}-5B-10}{4},\qquad
c=B+4-h.
$$

This $h$ is even, has 140 decimal digits, and is not divisible by three.
Removing powers of three therefore does not place it in either entry
test's left-coordinate range. The reset ancestor was only 294 counter
calls earlier and only 586 seed units below the basin.

The trajectory nevertheless returns to $A(1,B+644)$ after another 303
calls, hence halts by the basin theorem. The example shows that a recent
reset alone does not put the *first crossing state* in the small-primitive
criterion. It does not give a trajectory bypassing the basin.

## A quick return can feed an exceptional long excursion

Let $P=3^{292}$. Exact first returns give

$$
A(1,P+4137)\ \longrightarrow^{299}\ A(1,P+4744)
\ \longrightarrow^{1105}\ A(1,P+8153).
$$

The start $A(1,P+4139)$ has the same 299-call first-return destination.
The seed $P+4742$ is a sibling of $P+4744$: after their 292 opening
jumps, the first bulk merges their states. Each first excursion therefore
takes 1105 calls and ends at $P+8153$.

These origins lie below $H$. The 1105-call excursion exceeds the
[1086-call whole-population return budget](QUICK_RETURNS_758.md), yet
one of its sibling origins is the image of a 299-call return. Thus an
induction asserting that exceptional origins have no quick-return
predecessors is false. Excluding one representative of a merging sibling
pair would not exclude the common future either.

## The remaining obligation

A complete argument still needs a property of the selected blank
trajectory: for example, a certified connected passage to the basin, or
a bound on its actual reset-free excursions sufficient to force a reset
there. The examples above do not rule out such a property. They show why
it cannot be obtained from these two proposed general implications.


The checker uses literal counter calls, verifies that the displayed

examples are not used as witnesses for the blank orbit.

## Why rational-IFS periodicity does not close the ideal tail argument

The ideal survivor set is generated by

$$
f_0(x)=x/3,\qquad f_1(x)=(4x+5)/9.
$$

Reflection $R(x)=1-x$ gives
$R\circ f_0\circ R(y)=(y+2)/3$ and
$R\circ f_1\circ R(y)=4y/9$. This is exactly the IFS in
[MathOverflow question 496072](https://mathoverflow.net/questions/496072),
asked in June 2025: must its rational points have eventually periodic
addresses? The posted answer identifies a restricted base-$27/4$
subproblem and does not resolve the question.

For our eligible dyadics, the arithmetic obstruction to the usual proof
is explicit. If $x=N/4^p$ with $N$ odd and divisible by five, a forward
jump preserves its reduced denominator, whereas a forward pair gives

$$
P(x)=\frac{9N-5\cdot4^p}{4^{p+1}},
$$

whose numerator remains odd and divisible by five. Each pair therefore
multiplies the reduced denominator by exactly four. An infinite interior
survivor must contain infinitely many pairs: otherwise its final string
of jumps would eventually leave $[0,1]$. Its denominators would grow
without bound. An eventually periodic address determines an eventually
periodic forward orbit, since a repeated inverse word is a strict
contraction with a unique fixed point. Such a survivor's address
therefore could not be eventually periodic.

[Fishman and Simmons (2014), Lemma 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.2089)
proves rational-address periodicity when every contraction has numerator
$\pm1$. Its proof uses bounded denominators under the inverse branches.
[Schleischitz (2019), Theorem 4.3 and Section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.10689)
extends this to unimodular systems and lists the unrestricted version as
Problem 3. The multiplier $4/9$ does not satisfy either hypothesis.
Finite blocking does not repair this: any blocked word containing $p>0$
pairs has reduced multiplier $4^p/3^m$. The same paper's Section 3.2,
Theorem 3.4 and Problem 2, explains the analogous limitation for its
quantitative distance bound.

These observations provide neither an infinite dyadic survivor nor a
termination theorem. The selected dyadic class is narrower than the
full rational-point question, and no equivalence with the blank-machine
problem is asserted. Even termination of every ideal dyadic tail would
still require quantitative return bounds and physical guard-shadow
control before it could certify the needed machine excursions.
